\begin{abstract}
Hybrid language models replace most attention layers with linear-attention or state-space layers whose memory does not grow with context length, and several distillation recipes now convert a pretrained transformer into such a hybrid at modest cost. We study what this conversion does to associative recall at long context and whether the loss can be corrected after the fact. Using a synthetic suite in which 16 facts are planted in documents of 1K to 32K tokens, we compare two converted hybrids, Jet-Nemotron-2B and an 8B Mamba-in-the-Llama model, with the transformers they were converted from. Both hybrids score 1.00 on a single-needle test at every length up to 16K (0.86 and 1.00 at 32K), but at 32K they recall one of 16 planted facts at rates of 0.31 and 0.25, against 0.90 and 0.99 for their sources; RULER's multi-key tasks show the same gap. We then train a low-rank adapter on fewer than 1\% of the weights using synthetic many-fact documents, for about 40 minutes on a single GPU. Recall at 32K returns to the level of the source model (1.00 at 2B, 0.99 at 8B), replicated over three training seeds at each scale; the 8B adapter is trained only on documents up to 16K. The repair transfers to RULER's numeric multi-key tasks and to a held-out document template. A training-free alternative, copying the source transformer's query and key projections into the hybrid, does not recover recall at either scale. It is bounded by the coverage of its training data: multi-hop questions are repaired only once the training mix contains them, and UUID-valued keys and multi-answer questions, which the training data lacks, are not repaired. Collateral effects are small at 2B (+0.4\% bits per byte on long natural text, no change on ARC-Easy or HellaSwag) and depend on the training dose at 8B, where the 2B recipe over-fits (+15\%) and a lighter recipe gives full recall at +6\%. We release the evaluation harness, the training code and all raw outputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Full attention stores a key and value for every token in the context, so the memory needed to serve a request grows linearly with its length. Linear-attention and state-space layers instead maintain a state of fixed size, and hybrid architectures that keep a small number of full-attention layers alongside such layers now serve long contexts at a fraction of the memory cost. Because training a hybrid from scratch is expensive, several recipes convert an existing pretrained transformer into a hybrid by distillation \citep{mambainllama2024,lolcats2024,radlads2025,jetnemotron2025}. These papers evaluate the converted models mainly on short-context language-modelling benchmarks; long-context evaluation, where present, is a single-needle retrieval test or an aggregate score.

Two lines of prior work suggest that this evaluation may miss something. Models with a fixed-size recurrent state are known to lose associative recall as the number of stored facts and the distance to them grow \citep{zoology2023,based2024,waleffe2024,stuffedmamba2024}. And a single needle is the easiest case for such a state, which is the reason the RULER benchmark includes variants with several simultaneous needles \citep{ruler2024}.

This paper asks three questions about converted hybrids specifically.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Relative to the exact transformer it was converted from, does a converted hybrid lose multi-key recall at long context while still passing the single-needle test?
\item If so, is the deficit a capacity limit of the fixed-size state, or a training deficit? A small adapter trained after conversion distinguishes the two: if training restores recall, the capacity was present.
\item What does such a repair cost in general language-modelling ability, and what are its boundaries?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are as follows. (i) A paired measurement of two converted hybrids against their source transformers at 1K to 32K tokens, which shows a large multi-key recall deficit alongside perfect single-needle recall at both 2B and 8B scale (Figure~\ref{fig:signature}, Section~\ref{sec:results-signature}). (ii) A post-hoc low-rank adapter that restores multi-key recall at 32K to the level of the source model, replicated over three seeds at each scale, with a full-attention control trained on the same data, transfer to RULER and to a held-out template, generalisation past the training length, and a training-free baseline that copies the source's attention projections and fails (Section~\ref{sec:results-repair}). (iii) An analysis showing that the repair is bounded by the coverage of its training data, demonstrated for multi-hop questions, UUID values, multi-answer questions and natural-text replay (Section~\ref{sec:coverage}), together with a measurement of the collateral cost and a recipe that controls it at 8B (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Converting transformers into hybrids.} Mamba-in-the-Llama distils Llama-3 models into hybrids with a quarter or a half of their attention layers replaced by Mamba-2 layers, using the attention projections to initialise the new layers \citep{mambainllama2024,mamba2_2024}. LoLCATs linearises Llama and Mistral models by training linear attentions to mimic softmax attention, then correcting the approximation error with LoRA \citep{lolcats2024}. RADLADS converts Qwen2.5 models into RWKV-style recurrent decoders with a three-stage distillation \citep{radlads2025}. Jet-Nemotron selects which attention layers to keep with a search over a pretrained model, replaces the rest with a linear-attention block, and then trains the full model \citep{jetnemotron2025}. Lizard converts Llama-3-8B while keeping half of its softmax attention layers and reports RULER parity with its teacher up to 32K \citep{lizard2025}. The LoLCATs authors report that their 8B conversion loses passkey retrieval at 8K and recover it by using passkey data as the conversion data itself; LoLA later repairs single-needle retrieval in LoLCATs models at inference time with a sparse cache, while their multi-key RULER scores remain low \citep{lola2025}.

\paragraph{Recall in fixed-state models.} Zoology and Based measure associative recall in efficient language models and characterise the trade-off between recall and state size \citep{zoology2023,based2024}. An empirical study of Mamba-based models at 8B scale finds that pure Mamba models trail transformers on copying and phonebook lookup, and that adding a few attention layers recovers much of the gap \citep{waleffe2024}. Stuffed Mamba characterises state collapse beyond the training length and the state capacity for passkey retrieval \citep{stuffedmamba2024}. MiniMax report returning from a hybrid to full attention in their M2 model after observing deficits in multi-hop reasoning that small-scale benchmarks had not revealed \citep{minimaxm2_2025}.

\paragraph{Long-context evaluation.} RULER extends the single needle-in-a-haystack test with multi-key, multi-value and multi-query variants, variable tracking and aggregation tasks, and has become the standard synthetic evaluation of effective context length \citep{ruler2024}. An unpaired RULER aggregate has been reported for Jet-Nemotron-2B, falling to 46.6 at 32K \citep{jetlong2026}, and a third party reports Jet-Nemotron-2B at 100\% on single-needle retrieval at 32K \citep{attentionamnesia2026}. Neither work compares the converted model with the model it was converted from, and neither reports a per-task split.

\paragraph{Recall training and forgetting.} Continued training of from-scratch Mamba-transformer hybrids on recall-oriented data improves recall at a few thousand tokens with a small cost in commonsense accuracy; training on needle-in-a-haystack data specifically is reported to harm commonsense benchmarks \citep{hybridrecall2025}. In a different setting, fine-tuning retrieval components for LLM agents on synthetic data with LoRA causes forgetting on real and out-of-distribution data \citep{syntheticdatahurts2026}. Concurrently with this work, \citet{attentionamnesia2026} show that chain-of-thought fine-tuning degrades long-range recall in hybrids and attribute the damage to drift in the query and key projections, which they repair by restoring those projections from the pre-fine-tuning checkpoint. Our setting differs in that we repair a deficit introduced by conversion rather than by later fine-tuning, and we do so with a trained adapter; our observation that an adapter restricted to the query and key projections recovers most of the recall at the lowest cost is consistent with their explanation. Section~\ref{sec:restore} applies their training-free restore with the source transformer as the reference and finds that it does not carry over to the conversion setting.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Models}
We study two converted hybrids, each paired with the transformer it was converted from.
Jet-Nemotron-2B \citep{jetnemotron2025} has 28 blocks, of which 2 use full attention and 2 use sliding-window attention; the remaining 24 use the linear-attention JetBlock. It was initialised from Qwen2.5-1.5B with the MLPs frozen during the search stage; the final stage then trained the whole model for 350B tokens. Qwen2.5-1.5B is therefore the initialisation checkpoint rather than a weight-identical base, a caveat we return to in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
Mamba-in-the-Llama 8B is the released \texttt{Llama3.1-Mamba2-8B-dpo} checkpoint \citep{mambainllama2024}, in which 16 of the 32 layers of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{llama3_2024} are replaced by Mamba-2 layers \citep{mamba2_2024}. Its comparison model is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.
All models are run in bfloat16 with PyTorch's scaled-dot-product attention on NVIDIA A100-40GB GPUs, except the 8B replay experiments in Section~\ref{sec:cost}, which ran on A40 GPUs.

\subsection{Synthetic recall suite}
\label{sec:suite}
Each test document consists of filler text with planted sentences. Filler sentences are drawn deterministically from a combinatorial bank of $32^3$ sentences about office logistics and contain no digits. A planted sentence has the form \emph{The access code for locker KX-402 is 481930}; values are six-digit codes, chosen so that no two values in a document share their first three digits. Because the filler contains no digits, a six-digit number in a completion can only have been copied from a planted sentence or invented. Documents are sized under a single reference tokenizer (Qwen2.5) to targets of 1K, 2K, 4K, 8K, 16K and 32K tokens, landing within 1.3\% of the target; every model receives byte-identical prompts.

We use five tasks. \emph{Needle} plants a single fact, as in the standard needle-in-a-haystack test, and serves as the control. \emph{16 random keys} plants sixteen facts with unrelated keys and asks for one of them. \emph{16 similar keys} plants sixteen facts whose keys share a prefix and differ in their last digits (KX-401 to KX-416), which differ in a single token. \emph{Updated} plants eight facts, each later overwritten by a sentence that changes its value, and asks for the current value. \emph{Multi-hop} plants eight two-step chains (a locker is assigned to a person; the person has a code) and asks for the code of the person assigned to a given locker. A sixth task, \emph{parcels}, plants sixteen facts in a different template (\emph{Parcel 48213-K has the pickup code 731902}) and is used only for evaluation, never for training.

Prompts are completion-style, with no chat template: an instruction, the document, the question and the token \emph{Answer:}. Decoding is greedy with 32 new tokens; models whose tokenizer contains a \texttt{<think>} token are given the empty reasoning block as a prefix. A case is scored as recalled if the planted value appears among the six-digit numbers on the first non-empty line of the completion, after any reasoning block is removed. Unless stated otherwise, results use 25 cases per task and length in the initial comparison and 100 cases per task and length (two independently generated suites of 50) in the repair experiments.

\subsection{RULER}
To relate our suite to a standard benchmark we also run six needle variants from RULER \citep{ruler2024}, generated with the authors' released code and the same reference tokenizer: single needle in a noise haystack, three multi-key variants (four distractor keys in an essay haystack; a haystack composed of needles; UUID keys and values), multi-value and multi-query, with 50 cases per task at 16K and 32K. Scoring follows RULER's rule: a case is correct if every expected string appears anywhere in the completion.

\subsection{Repair procedure}
\label{sec:repair-setup}
The repair data are synthetic documents of the same construction as the suite, restricted to the locker template. Each document plants 16 to 48 facts of one of four kinds, cycled evenly: random keys, near-duplicate keys, keys overwritten once, and two-hop chains, and ends with eight questions and their answers. Our first experiments used only the first three kinds; the fourth was added after the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:coverage}. The training loss is the cross-entropy of the answer tokens only. Because a 16K-token document with a 128K-token vocabulary produces 8~GiB of logits, the output head is applied only at the answer positions, which keeps training within a single 40~GB GPU.

We train a LoRA adapter \citep{lora2021} of rank 16 and scaling 32 on every linear projection outside the MLPs, that is, the attention projections and the projections of the linear-attention or Mamba-2 layers; the embeddings, output head and MLPs are frozen. At 2B this wraps 232 layers and 0.75\% of the parameters; at 8B, 96 layers and 0.15\%. Optimisation uses AdamW with four documents per step and gradient checkpointing. We report two training doses. The \emph{heavy} recipe uses 600 documents (150 steps) at learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$; the \emph{gentle} recipe uses 300 documents at $5\times10^{-5}$. The 2B model is trained on documents of 8K, 16K and 32K tokens; the 8B model only on 8K and 16K, so that its results at 32K measure generalisation beyond the training length. The heavy 2B run processes 11.5M tokens in 42 minutes on one A100; the gentle 8B run processes 3.7M tokens. After training the adapter is merged into the weights, so inference cost is unchanged; merged and unmerged evaluation give identical scores.

\subsection{Controls and collateral measurements}
To separate repair from mere familiarity with the prompt format, the same recipe is applied to the full-attention source model at 2B; a gain that appears in both models reflects the format, a gain only in the hybrid reflects repair. To measure collateral effects we report ARC-Easy and HellaSwag accuracy on 500 validation items each (standard error 0.022), and bits per byte on eight windows of 16K tokens from a long natural text (\emph{Moby-Dick}), before and after repair. Generation was found to be deterministic: 150 of 150 completions on the held-out suite were byte-identical between two independent runs on the same GPU type.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Converted hybrids lose multi-key recall while passing the needle test}
\label{sec:results-signature}
Figure~\ref{fig:signature} plots recall against context length for both pairs in the initial sweep of 25 cases per point; Table~\ref{tab:signature} gives the values at 32K from the larger evaluation of 100 cases per cell, together with RULER's multi-key tasks. The numbers quoted in this paragraph are from the 100-case evaluation. All four models score 1.00 on the single-needle test at every length up to 16K; at 32K the sources and the 8B hybrid remain at 1.00 and Jet-Nemotron-2B scores 0.86 (1.00 in the 25-case sweep). On sixteen planted facts the sources remain near their short-context level, whereas the hybrids degrade with length. At 2B the gap opens at 16K (0.57 against 0.90) and reaches 0.59 at 32K; at 8B it opens at 4K and is near total at 32K, where the hybrid recalls one of sixteen facts at 0.25 against 0.99 for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The misses are well formed: in every case we inspected, the model returned another planted code in a grammatical answer.

RULER's multi-key tasks detect the same failure (Table~\ref{tab:signature}), so our suite adds no detection power over RULER; its role here is to provide the paired measurement and the training signal. Two further observations run against our expectations. Near-duplicate keys were no harder than random keys below 32K, so key geometry is not the dominant stressor at this scale. And the 8B conversion, which keeps half of its attention layers, loses recall earlier and more completely than the 2B conversion, which keeps two of 28; the number of retained attention layers does not by itself predict the deficit.

\subsection{A small adapter restores recall}
\label{sec:results-repair}
Figure~\ref{fig:repair} and Table~\ref{tab:repair} show recall at 32K before and after repair. At 2B, three seeds of the heavy recipe raise recall on sixteen random keys from 0.31 to 1.00, 0.98 and 1.00, and on sixteen similar keys from 0.22 to 1.00, 0.98 and 1.00. The full-attention control trained on the same data reaches 1.00 and 0.96, so the repaired hybrid matches the trained source. At 8B, three seeds of the gentle recipe raise recall on random keys from 0.25 to 1.00, 1.00 and 0.99, and on similar keys from 0.09 to 0.99, 1.00 and 0.99, matching the untrained Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. These 8B adapters were trained only on documents of 8K and 16K tokens, so the repair generalises beyond the training length.

The repair also transfers. On the held-out parcel template, which differs from the training data in wording and key format, recall at 32K rises from 0.45 to 0.93--0.98 at 2B and from 0.17 to 0.98--1.00 at 8B. On RULER, whose prompts use a different instruction, an essay haystack and seven-digit values, the 8B hybrid's multi-key scores at 32K rise from 0.40 and 0.10 to 0.96--1.00 and 0.86--0.90 on the first two variants, the level of the source model; at 2B they rise from 0.56 and 0.26 to 0.81 and 0.49 (pooled over seeds), against 0.94 and 0.72 for the source and 0.96 and 0.97 for the trained control.

We conclude that the deficit is a training deficit rather than a capacity limit: the fixed-size state can hold sixteen facts over 32K tokens, and a few million tokens of targeted training supply the retrieval the conversion did not.

\subsection{A training-free baseline: copying the source's attention projections}
\label{sec:restore}
Attention Amnesia \citep{attentionamnesia2026} repairs recall lost during chain-of-thought fine-tuning without training: it copies the query and key projections of the retained attention layers back from the checkpoint before fine-tuning. A converted hybrid has no such checkpoint, but the source transformer is the natural candidate, since the retained attention layers were initialised from it. We therefore copied the source's query and key weights and biases into every retained softmax-attention layer of each hybrid (four layers at 2B, two full-attention and two sliding-window; sixteen at 8B) and ran the same evaluation. This follows their Algorithm~1 and released code, which copy the query and key weights and biases of each listed softmax-attention layer, with the source transformer in place of the pre-fine-tuning checkpoint; neither model has query or key normalisation.

The copy repairs neither model. At 2B it removes retrieval altogether: recall is 0.00 on every task at 16K and 32K (100 cases per cell); on 93\% of the 1,200 cases, the single needle included, the model answers with a sentence saying the answer is not in the text, and on the rest with a repetition loop; no completion contains a six-digit code. The reason is visible in the weights. Jet-Nemotron-2B's softmax-attention projections have cosine similarity below 0.01 with Qwen2.5-1.5B's at the same layer, at any other layer and under any pairing of heads, while its MLPs (0.71) and embeddings (0.88) still resemble the source: the attention layers were trained anew during conversion, so no reference checkpoint exists for them. The method's own Jet-Nemotron-2B experiment, with the released checkpoint as the reference, also recovers only part of the fine-tuning loss (their NIAH-Single-2 at 32K: 57.8 to 64.6, against 95.6 before fine-tuning). At 8B the retained attention layers do stay close to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's (cosine 0.98--0.99 on the query and key projections), yet copying them lowers recall at 32K on sixteen random keys from 0.25 to 0.04 and on the single needle from 1.00 to 0.72 (56--60 cases per cell; at 16K, with 100 cases, 0.07 and 0.67), where the adapter gives 1.00 on both. The sixteen attention layers have adapted to the Mamba-2 layers between them, and the source's projections no longer fit. The contrast with Section~\ref{sec:cost}, where an adapter restricted to the same query and key projections but trained on the recall data recovers most of the deficit, locates the repair in the training rather than in the choice of parameters.


\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{The repair is bounded by the coverage of its training data}
\label{sec:coverage}
Across the experiments we found four cases consistent with the adapter restoring only the kind of retrieval present in its training data. The positive direction, that adding a question type to the training mix repairs it, was tested for multi-hop questions; for UUID values and multi-answer questions we observe only the negative direction.

\emph{Multi-hop questions.} Our first training mix contained only one-hop questions. The resulting 2B adapter answered two-hop questions with the person's name, that is, the one-hop value adjacent to the locker key, in 124 of 150 cases, and multi-hop recall fell from 0.20 to 0.03 while the control's rose from 0.16 to 0.31. Adding two-hop chains to the mix raised multi-hop recall to 0.86 at 2B and 0.99 at 8B.

\emph{UUID values.} The training data contains only numeric values. RULER's third multi-key variant, with UUID keys and values, barely moves: from 0.16 to 0.19 at 2B and from 0.00 to 0.30 at 8B, against 0.40 and 0.96 for the sources.

\emph{Multi-answer questions.} Every training question has exactly one answer. RULER's multi-value and multi-query tasks recover only partly in the hybrids, and in the trained 2B control they become worse (multi-value at 16K from 0.74 to 0.57).

\emph{Natural-text replay.} Interleaving windows of natural text with ordinary language-modelling loss, a standard remedy for forgetting, removed the perplexity cost at 8B (Section~\ref{sec:cost}) but reduced multi-hop recall from 0.99 to 0.30--0.36 in all three replay variants we ran, while single-fact recall stayed near 1.00. The task requiring the most retrieval signal is the one that is lost when half of the training signal is replaced.

A practical repair must therefore contain the question types and value types of the intended workload. The parameter budget of the adapter is small, but its data must be complete.

\subsection{Collateral cost and the training dose}
\label{sec:cost}
At 2B the collateral effects are small. ARC-Easy and HellaSwag accuracy change by less than one standard error for all three seeds, and bits per byte on long natural text rise from 1.082 to 1.084--1.087 (+0.4\%); the trained control pays the same.

At 8B the same recipe over-fits: the training loss reaches zero, bits per byte rise by 15\% (from 1.008 to 1.156) and HellaSwag falls by 3 to 5 points. Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} and Table~\ref{tab:recipes} compare the recipes we tried. Reducing the dose to 300 documents at a quarter of the learning rate (the gentle recipe) gives full recall with bits per byte at +5.5\% to +7.4\% and benchmarks within noise, consistently over three seeds. Restricting the adapter to the query and key projections reduces the cost to +1.8\% with no benchmark change; this variant recalls well on our suite but transfers least to RULER. Replay lowers bits per byte below the unrepaired model at the cost described above. We recommend the gentle recipe as the default and report its cost rather than claiming none.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
We evaluate two conversion families at two scales; other conversions may behave differently, and Lizard's reported RULER parity \citep{lizard2025} shows that the deficit is not universal. Qwen2.5-1.5B is the initialisation checkpoint of Jet-Nemotron-2B rather than a weight-identical base, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct likewise served as initialisation for the 8B hybrid, so part of each gap may predate the attention replacement; the repair results do not depend on this attribution. The 8B recipe comparison uses one training seed per recipe except for the gentle recipe. Our collateral measurements are ARC-Easy, HellaSwag and bits per byte on one book; we do not evaluate a natural long-context task such as document question answering. UUID-valued keys are not repaired by any recipe we tried. Finally, our suite is synthetic; whether the repaired recall carries over to retrieval from real documents is not tested here.

\section{Conclusion}
Converted hybrid language models can pass the single-needle test at 32K while recalling one of sixteen planted facts at a quarter of the rate of the transformers they were converted from. A low-rank adapter trained for under an hour on synthetic many-fact documents restores recall to the source model's level at both 2B and 8B, generalises past its training length, and transfers to RULER and to an unseen template. The repair is bounded by the coverage of its training data: multi-hop questions were repaired only once the training mix contained them, and UUID values and multi-answer questions, absent from the training data, were not repaired by any recipe we tried. Copying the source transformer's attention projections, the training-free route, does not work in this setting: at 2B those layers were trained anew during conversion, and at 8B they have adapted to their new neighbours. Its cost is small at 2B and controllable at 8B. These results suggest that conversion pipelines should include a recall-repair stage, and that any such stage should be built from the question and value types of the workload it is meant to serve.
